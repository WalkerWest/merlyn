Regularis Order Build

Vendors pay you to order more.
Order the right more.

Bearing vendors reward bigger orders with a discount, free freight, or a rebate at year end. To get there, you add parts to the order. Order Build tells you which parts to add: the ones you will sell soon. And which to leave off: the ones that would sit on the shelf for years. When a vendor announces a price increase, it tells you which parts are worth buying before it.

Your ERP's suggested order already does some of this. Here is where it stops.

Min/max, or order point and line point, was built for parts that sell every week, and it does that job well. Most of a bearing catalog sells a few times a year.

The line point fills out the order with whatever is below its max, however long it will sit.

Order Build prices every extra unit by how long it will sit, and leaves off the ones that cost more to hold than they earn.

A single part that hits its order point ships on its own, and misses the freight minimum.

Order Build gathers those parts into the vendor's order, where they help reach the minimum instead of paying freight alone.

The vendor's discount tiers, freight minimum and rebate are invisible to it.

Order Build weighs the deal on every order, and says when chasing it doesn't pay.

What else min/max misses, such as sales lost because a part was out, is in What your history adds.

        How it decides

        The same arithmetic a good buyer does in their head, done for every part at once.

        Restock what's needed now

        Every part a branch is short of gets ordered. Those parts would be bought anyway, so they cost nothing extra to hold.

        Price every extra part by how long it will sit

        An extra unit of a part that sells every month sits about a month. One that sells every two years sits about two years, and likely longer: a slow seller's rate is uncertain, so it is charged for that too. The longer it sits, the more it costs to hold.

        Pick the order that leaves the most money

        It tries every order size, adds up the discount, freight and rebate each one earns and any price rise it gets ahead of, takes off the holding cost, and keeps the best. Then it shows you why, part by part.

        It is a guide, not a guarantee. "Sells about once a year" is an average, and a part can go longer. Parts bought now count toward this year's rebate instead of next year's. The buyer always has the last word.

        What your history adds

        The page above works from one sales number per part, with an allowance for how uncertain a slow seller's number is. Most of a bearing catalog sells a few times a year or less, and that is where a single number is weakest. With two to three years of your sales history, the same tool gets sharper in six ways.

        A truer sales rate

        Sales history is cleaned before it is counted. Lost sales are added back, because a sale you could not make is still demand; min/max counts only what sold, so a part that runs out looks like it stopped selling, its order point drops, and it stays out. One-time big orders are set aside, so a single project does not look like a trend. Parts with thin history borrow from interchangeable parts and from the same part at your other branches.

        Judgment, measured

        Scored adjustments to the forecast

        Each outside factor gets two scores: its effect on demand, from −3 to +3, and its supply risk, from 0 to 3, each with a confidence, a source and a review-by date. Each product group has a sensitivity to each factor: 0 for none, 1 for normal, 2 for high. Farm signals reach farm parts; tariffs reach imported lines hardest.

        demand change, group g  = Σ over factors f of
    score[f] · %/point[f] · confidence[f] · sensitivity[f,g]
                                               (capped)
lead-time change        = the same, from supply risk (capped)

adjusted rate = rate without season
              · (1 + demand change) · season index

        Two safeguards. Trend is measured against the same months last year, so a seasonal part is not counted twice: once by its trend going into the season, and again by its seasonal index. And every factor is tested, the forecast with it against the forecast without it, on demand it has not seen; a factor that does not help loses its weight. A score past its review date stops counting until someone reviews it.

        Key customers get a public-records health check (status, bankruptcy, judgments, liens). A customer at risk lowers the demand behind its parts, and one that has stopped buying releases the stock held for it.

        Forecasting

        Intermittent demand: Croston and SBA

        An ordinary moving average treats a part that sells 0, 0, 0, 3, 0, 0 as selling half a unit a month. It is never right: too high in the empty months, too low in the month that matters. Croston's method splits the history into two things and smooths each separately: how big a sale is when one happens, and how long the gaps between sales are.

        when a sale of size y happens, q periods after the last one:
  size      z ← z + α(y − z)
  interval  p ← p + α(q − p)
rate per period   f = z / p
bias-corrected    f = (1 − α/2) · z / p     (Syntetos-Boylan)

        Croston's original estimate runs high; the correction removes most of that bias. Parts are first sorted by how often they sell and how much sale sizes vary (average gap above 1.32 periods, squared coefficient of variation above 0.49), and each group gets the method that suits it. A further variant, by Teunter, Syntetos and Babai, updates the chance of a sale every period, so it notices when a part has stopped selling, which matters for obsolete bearings.

        Uncertain demand

        Gamma-Poisson: charging for what we don't know

        Sales of a part are modeled as random arrivals at some rate λ a year (a Poisson process). For a slow seller, λ itself is uncertain, so it gets a distribution too: a gamma distribution, which updates neatly as sales come in.

        before the part's own history:  λ ~ Gamma(a, b)      (from its family)
after n units sold in T years:  λ ~ Gamma(a + n, b + T)

expected wait for the k-th extra unit:
  E[k / λ] = k (b + T) / (a + n − 1)

        The point is that the average wait is longer than k divided by the average rate, because a slow rate stretches the wait far more than a fast one shortens it. Example: a part that sold once last year, in a family that averages about one sale a year (a = 2, b = 2). Its expected rate is still one a year, but an extra unit's expected wait is 1.5 years, not 1. That is 50% more holding cost, charged to exactly the parts most likely to sit.

        The tool on this page works this way. Each part's starting point comes from its interchange family on the order, if your file names one and the family has at least three parts, and otherwise from the whole order, fitted loosely so it adds uncertainty without moving a part's own average much. With your full history, the family is drawn from your whole catalog instead.

        Lumpy demand

        Negative binomial: parts that sell in sets

        When customers buy several at once, demand is two random things: how many orders arrive, and how many units each order takes. Orders arriving at random with sizes from a logarithmic distribution add up to a negative binomial; in general it is a compound Poisson.

        orders  N ~ Poisson(μ)
sizes   S₁, S₂, ...   (fitted from order lines)
units   D = S₁ + ... + Sₙ
mean    E[D] = μ · E[S]
spread  Var[D] / E[D] = E[S²] / E[S]
        (above 1 once orders exceed one unit)

        Two consequences. Lead-time demand is wider than a one-at-a-time model says, which changes how many units a branch needs to cover a breakdown order of four. And extra stock clears in jumps, with longer gaps between them, so it waits longer than the same yearly total sold one at a time. The negative binomial is also what the gamma-Poisson model predicts for future counts, so one family of distributions covers both effects.

        Sparse data

        Pooling across families and branches

        Most part-and-branch pairs have too few sales to estimate anything alone. So each borrows strength from groups it belongs to: its interchange family (the same bearing from other makers, with equivalent suffixes, and any part it supersedes), and the same part at your other branches. The family's history sets the starting point; the part's own history moves it.

        blended rate = (a + n) / (b + T)
             = w · (n / T)  +  (1 − w) · (a / b)
weight on the part's own history   w = T / (b + T)

        A part with long, busy history follows its own numbers; a part with one sale leans on its family. The family prior (a, b) is fitted from the spread of rates within the family, so a tight family pulls hard and a loose one barely pulls at all. Branch-level demand then comes from the family total times each branch's share. This needs clean interchange and supersession tables, which is often the first real piece of work.

        Optimization

        The network stocking model

        The stocking model decides how many of each part every branch should hold, all at once, because the decisions interact: one unit at a hub can cover a breakdown at a neighbor by transfer, and every unit competes for the same inventory budget and shelf space. It is a mixed-integer program, solved with Google OR-Tools (CP-SAT).

        choose   x[i,b,k] = 1 if part i is stocked at level k at branch b
value    of the k-th unit: margin × P(lead-time demand ≥ k)
         + cover it gives nearby branches − cost to hold it
subject  one level per part and branch;  budget and space by branch
         and in total;  required stock for customer commitments

        The demand distributions from the other notes feed the value term, which is why they matter: the chance a unit is needed comes straight from them. The result is a target for every part at every branch, which is what "needed now" should be measured against. On this page, the order build itself is a different, smaller problem: an exact search over order totals with the discount, freight and rebate steps, solved by dynamic programming right in your browser.
